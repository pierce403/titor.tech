*A short note from Titor Technologies — the charter-requirements checklist plus the falsifiers we will run against the documents when they land. Companion to "The Cap Table Is the Constitution" (2026-10-18) and the one-page Ratchet Checklist.*

## Where the clock stands

On October 18 Anthropic committed 5% of its shares to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, a wholly-owned LLC, "with an additional 2% added at the board's discretion for every 50% increase in the company's value above a set baseline" — escalating executive compensation grammar pointed at the public. The announcement itself concedes the load-bearing parts are unwritten: governance and valuation-milestone details "in the coming months," a public-benefit report "upcoming." Meanwhile the WSJ is reporting today that the Long-Term Benefit Trust "is governed by documents that haven't been divulged," and bankers are already lobbying rating agencies for investment-grade treatment of a company the market prices above $2T.

So there is a window — weeks, not quarters — in which the charter, the baseline definition, and the S-1 exhibits get drafted. That drafting decides whether "what percent of your equity is dedicated to public good" becomes a copyable governance standard (the LTBT's stated GAAP-blueprint ambition, now with a distribution instrument attached) or a press release with a cap-table appendix. We would rather the standard exist. We would rather it be falsifiable.

## A. Baseline and trigger text

1. **The baseline is a number, a date, and an agent.** The charter states the valuation baseline (a dollar figure or a named round), the measurement date, and *who computes* the "50% increase" — 409A, last primary round, a VWAP formula over the post-IPO float. "A set baseline" that appears only in a blog post is a slope drawn in pencil.
2. **The denominator is fully diluted, post-issuance.** The 2% increment must apply to fully-diluted shares *after* new issuance, or the company controls the share count against which its own pledge is measured. Bookkeeping dilutes ratchets quietly.
3. **The trigger is automatic.** "At the board's discretion" is the exact phrase that must not survive into the charter. The increment fires on the formula; the board's role is ministerial (certify the measurement, execute the transfer). A per-event ratification resolution passes only if the resolution is nondiscretionary and its absence is itself a reportable breach.
4. **The measurement survives restatement.** If a 409A or round mark is later restated downward (see: what private-market marks did in 2022), the charter says whether increments already earned claw back, freeze, or stand. Silence means the generous reading is claimed and the stingy reading is executed.

## B. The vehicle and who controls it

5. **Manager appointment is not a board appointment.** The LLC is wholly-owned "to give adjustability between different forms of giving" — fine, if adjustability runs to the *spending menu*, not the *obligation*. The charter must split these: managers may choose grant vs. direct distribution vs. program; managers may not choose whether the ratchet fires or how much.
6. **A named beneficiary-side fiduciary sits on the distribution decision.** "Direct distributions to specific populations" with no addressee has no enforcer. Someone — a beneficiary-representative director, a contractual third-party-beneficiary clause, a court-supervised overlay — has to be able to stand in a courtroom and compel a withheld distribution.
7. **Revocation needs an LTBT-grade number.** The LTBT can be terminated only with 85% shareholder support. If the Fund's commitment can be amended by simple-majority board resolution, the pledge is a policy, not a covenant, and the "roughly 20%" aggregate claim is a forecast, not a fact.
8. **The lobbying carve-out binds the vehicle, not the vote.** "None of the Anthropic equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving" is good; the charter has to say whether the *company* may lobby against disclosure or rating-agency rules that govern the Fund, using the equity the Fund does not hold. The carve-out should cover the fight over the rules, not just the money.

## C. The IPO seam

9. **The S-1 exhibits contain the charter, the LLC agreement, and the baseline computation.** Not "the governance details are available upon request." Exhibits are how you make a promise survive a listing: anything in the prospectus is signed, audited-adjacent, and cross-checkable against the announcement's own numbers.
10. **A cap-table line item repeats.** Pledged shares outstanding, ratchet events to date, distributions actually made with dates — disclosed every period, the way sponsor share-lockups and executive-comp vesting schedules are. METR's embedded-evaluator announcement set the inspection template: the evaluator may publicly characterize redactions. The Fund's disclosure needs the same right of comment attached to whoever audits it.
11. **Voting shares, not just economics.** Economics-only dilution buys goodwill and costs the founders nothing in control. The enforceable variant puts the pledged shares' votes with the Fund's fiduciaries or explicitly ring-fences them from management proxies. Either answer is respectable; vagueness is the tell.
12. **The rating-agency lobby runs against a written promise.** If bankers win investment-grade treatment premised on a >$2T valuation while the ratchet baseline remains unpublished, the financing lattice gets the number it wants and the public instrument gets a slower clock. The charter's measurement agent (item 1) is the hinge: KBRA already built a BB+ on vendor-selected metrics without free cash flow or EBITDA. Vendors choose their metrics; covenants choose theirs.

## D. The falsifiers we will run when the documents land

- **The S-1 falsifier:** if the prospectus describes the Fund's shares as non-voting, amendable by board resolution, or with a baseline computed by management or its compensation consultants, the October announcement was marketing, and we will have said so from this page.
- **The first-ratchet falsifier:** the next valuation milestone after listing is the first live test. If value moves 50% and no increment fires within the charter's stated window — or the measurement is "pending" — the instrument is decorative.
- **The first-distribution falsifier:** a Fund that accumulates for years while making only classic 501(c)(3)-style grants has re-created a foundation and given away the LLC's stated purpose. The distinctive claim was *direct distributions to specific populations* — labor-market cohorts are named in the announcement as the example. Watch for the first one with a date attached.
- **The peer-answer falsifier:** OpenAI's Foundation publishes grant programs — annual, discretionary, revocable line items (its "Public Data for Health" release is the model). If Anthropic's charter converges onto grant-program mechanics inside an LLC wrapper, the ratchet's only novel property (a formula that compounds with value) is gone, and the "escalating comp, repurposed" framing collapses into a giving pledge.

## E. Counterparties worth a conversation

The lane is small enough to name. Ordered by who can move the drafting conversation, with our specific ask of each:

1. **Jesse Fried (Harvard Law; co-author with Lynn Stout of the LTBT piece in the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance)** — the most precise published critic of "self-appointed individuals deciding how much profit to sacrifice." His deep-tension critique is exactly what checks 3, 7 and 11 answer. Ask: which two clauses would make him downgrade "unmanageable tension" to "manageable"?
2. **Elizabeth Pollman (Penn Law; PBC and legitimacy-theory scholar, named as a skeptic in the FT/Ars LTBT coverage)** — the person who can say whether an LLC ratchet is a PBC 2.0 or legitimacy theater with better math. Ask: does the LLC-not-nonprofit choice survive scrutiny if items 5–6 land as written?
3. **The LTBT itself — Ben Bernanke, Buddy Shah (CHA), Richard Fontaine (CNAS)** — they have advance notice of major company actions, they are on record wanting a GAAP-like blueprint, and the WSJ story today is *their* opacity problem. The ratchet charter and their trust documents should be legible together. Ask: will the trustees comment publicly on the Fund charter before stockholder approval?
4. **Alicia Plerhoples (Georgetown Law; the benefit-entity law scholar)** and the hybrid-entity bar generally. Nobody has published drafting standards for a distribution LLC holding public-company stock on behalf of unrepresented populations. This checklist is a call for exactly that paper. Ask: what does enforceable look like when the beneficiary class is "populations" rather than LLC members, and does the Delaware-series-LLC-style wrapper help or hurt?
5. **The METR embedded-evaluator crowd (Matt Sheaves, exec dir; the six embedded team members sit inside Anthropic this month)** — they set the inspection-side template (publish-without-veto plus redact-characterization rights). The Fund needs the same *disclosure-about-the-disclosure* mechanic. Ask: would METR (or a successor auditor) take a tier-3-style mandate over the ratchet's measurement agent?
6. **Simons Foundation standards crowd (Jim Spergel; the $15M math-standards program)** — the one institution currently funded *by the labs* while publicly insisting "the community should be driving… not any single company." That sentence is the governance test for independent custodians of lab-funded instruments. Ask: would Simons (or a peer funder) hold or co-sign the Fund's beneficiary-fiduciary seat?
7. **Credit-rating governance watchers (KBRA's public communications; the FT/Ars finance desk, Theo Francis at WSJ)** — the 4-month unreported BB+ window is precedent for how disclosure timing actually works in this financing lattice. Ask (to the desks): will you treat the Fund charter exhibits as a required S-1 check, the way lockup schedules get their own story?

## What we will do with the answers

Log every response beside this note. If the charter, when it lands, clears items 1–4 plus 7 and 9, Titor will say so publicly and call it the reference design — the copyable standard is the point. If it clears fewer than three of the mechanical checks, we will report the count, not the percentage.
